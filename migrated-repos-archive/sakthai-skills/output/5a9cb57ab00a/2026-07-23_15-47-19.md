## SeededGrasp: Language-Guided Grasping in Complex Scenes with Multiple Embodiments

**Paper:** [arxiv.org/abs/2607.20207](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20207) | **Project:** [uoft-isl.github.io/seeded-grasp](https://uoft-isl.github.io/seeded-grasp/)
**Authors:** Yang Xu, Gurpreet Singh Mukker, Raymond Wang, Jasper Gerik, Maria Attarian, Igor Gilitschenski (University of Toronto)

**The build-vs-buy problem in robot grasping.** Tell a robot "pick up the red mug" and two things have to happen: the high-level semantic understanding ("red mug" → that object over there), and the low-level geometric execution (where and how to grip so it doesn't fall). Existing approaches typically either ask a VLM to predict the full grasp — which demands spatial reasoning the VLM wasn't trained for — or they train the VLM jointly with the grasping model, which is enormously data-hungry and ties you to one robot morphology. SeededGrasp says: don't do either. Decouple them.

**A seed point as the information bottleneck.** Their architecture is elegantly simple: a VLM (no fine-tuning needed) looks at the scene and language instruction, and outputs a single 3D *seed point* — roughly "grasp somewhere around here." That seed point then conditions a lightweight, embodiment-specific grasp-generation model that handles the geometric details. The VLM doesn't need to know wrist angles, and the grasping model doesn't need to understand "red mug." This means the same VLM can drive different robot arms by swapping only the grasp-generation head — no retraining the language side. They release the first multi-embodiment tabletop grasping dataset (2.5M grasps in cluttered scenes) to enable this.

**The numbers and why they matter.** 72% success in simulation, 78% in real-world — competitive with end-to-end approaches at a fraction of the training cost. But the real signal here is architectural: the modular seed-point design lets you upgrade either the VLM or the grasp generator independently as better models come out, without touching the other piece. For a field where robot hardware churns faster than research software, that decoupling is more valuable than a few percentage points of grasp success. The multi-embodiment dataset release also matters — most grasping datasets are tied to one arm, which has made cross-embodiment work hard to reproduce.
